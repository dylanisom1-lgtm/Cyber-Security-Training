CRYPTOGRAPHY · PART 1/3

Part 1 — What Actually Makes a Password Strong

Start with a padlock

Forget passwords for a second and picture a combination padlock — the kind with little wheels you spin.

A cheap one has 3 wheels, each showing the digits 0–9. How many combinations does a thief have to try, worst case? Ten options on the first wheel, ten on the second, ten on the third: 10 × 10 × 10 = 1,000. Tedious, but a patient thief gets there.

Now change one thing at a time and watch what happens:

  • Add a fourth wheel → 10 × 10 × 10 × 10 = 10,000. Ten times harder, from a single extra wheel.
  • Instead, put letters on the original three wheels (36 symbols each instead of 10) → 36 × 36 × 36 = 46,656. Forty-six times harder, without adding a wheel at all.

That's the whole idea of password strength, and there's nothing more to it than those two levers: how many symbols each position can hold, and how many positions there are. A password is just a padlock with a lot of wheels.

The Three Variables

Everything about cracking a password comes down to one back-of-the-napkin relationship:

charset ^ length ÷ attacker speed = time to crack

Three variables, and it's worth naming them because we'll spend the rest of this module watching things push them around:

Variable What it is The padlock version
V1 Character set How many different symbols each position can hold Symbols on each wheel
V2 Length How many positions there are Number of wheels
V3 Attacker speed How many guesses per second the attacker can make How fast the thief can spin

Let's put real numbers in, using a concrete worked example. Take an 8-character password with lower-case, upper-case, and digits. That's a character set of 62 (26 + 26 + 10), to the power of 8:

62 ⁸ ≈ 218,000,000,000,000 possible passwords

Divide that by a fast cracking rig's speed — about 76.5 billion guesses per second — and you get roughly 2,853 seconds. About 47 minutes. A password most people would call "pretty strong" falls in under an hour.

Hold onto that 47-minute figure. It's going to move a lot.

The one idea that ties the whole module together

Here's the framing to carry with you: almost every security concept you'll ever meet is just a move on one of those three variables. Once you see it, you can't un-see it.

The thing What it's really doing
A minimum-length rule Pushes V2 up — and V2 is the exponent, so it's the strongest lever you have
A complexity rule (must include a symbol) Pushes V1 up
Salting (adding random bits) Pushes V1 / V2 up by adding length and unpredictability
A slow hashing algorithm (bcrypt) on the server Pushes V3 down — deliberately slowing the attacker by orders of magnitude
A broken hash like MD5 Pushes V3 up — the reason that 47-minute crack was so fast
Passwordless login (passkeys) Drives V3 toward zero — there's no password to crack at all
Multi-factor authentication Sits outside the formula entirely — a second lock that doesn't care about the first one's math

And it's not only technical controls. Shoulder-surfing — someone glancing at your screen and catching that your password starts with a capital A — just knocked one wheel off the padlock. That's V2 dropping by one. Watch what a single character does to our example: 47 minutes becomes 46 seconds. One glance. That's why "cover your screen" is a real control and not just etiquette.

See it run

You've got the formula on paper. Now watch it run on real passwords.

hands-on · Password Cracker Simulation

Play for a few minutes; the simulation is this formula executing. Two things to try on purpose: 1. Change the attacker model (throttled login → stolen-database GPU rig → nation-state cluster). The password never changes, but the crack time swings from years to seconds. That's V3 moving, and nothing else. 2. Feed it a word, then a random string of the same length. Biscuit1991! looks like it has a big V1 and V2 — mixed case, a number, a symbol, twelve characters. But the cracker doesn't treat it as 12 random positions; it treats it as one dictionary word plus a predictable decoration, and cracks it almost instantly. A random string of the same length holds for millennia.

back to the lesson

What you just saw. Two lessons, both of which the rest of the module leans on. First, V3 is the attacker's to move, not yours — which is why "was our password database stolen?" matters more than "was my password strong?" Second, and quieter: the formula only protects you if every position is genuinely unpredictable. Predictability is a hidden discount on V1 and V2. The moment your password follows a human pattern, the attacker stops brute-forcing and starts guessing smart — and the math stops being on your side.

So that's why the policy says what it says

If you've ever rolled your eyes at a password policy, this is where it stops being arbitrary:

  • "At least 14 characters." They're buying exponent — the biggest lever there is (V2).
  • "Don't reuse passwords across sites." One breach shouldn't hand an attacker a free guess everywhere else. This defeats the leaked-list shortcut before the formula even runs.
  • "Use the password manager." A manager generates genuinely random strings, which is the only way to actually get the V1 and V2 you think you have.
  • "We require MFA." The safety net for the day a password does fall — the lock outside the formula.

None of those rules are IT being difficult. Each one is a deliberate push on a variable you can now name.

CRYPTOGRAPHY · PART 2/3

Part 2 — What the Server Actually Stores

Pick up where the cracker left off

In Part 1, the attacker ground through billions of guesses to break a password. But look closely at what they were attacking. They never had your password — they were attacking its stored form, guessing inputs and checking each one against something the server had on file.

So what does the server have on file? Here's the reassuring part, and the whole subject of this part: a well-built server never stores your actual password. It stores a hash of it. To understand why that's safe — and why the attacker had to work so hard — we need to understand what a hash actually is.

A hash is a fingerprint for data

Think of a hash function as a machine that takes any input — a single word, a contract, an entire movie file — and stamps out a fingerprint of it. That fingerprint has four properties, and every one of them maps to something you already know about real fingerprints:

  • Deterministic. The same finger always makes the same print. Run "password" through the machine a thousand times and you get the identical result every time. This is how login works: the server stores the fingerprint of your password, and when you sign in it fingerprints what you typed and checks whether the two match — without ever knowing or keeping the password itself.
  • Fixed length. A fingerprint fits on the same little card whether it came from a giant or a child. A hash is the same: one word in, or a whole novel in, and the output is always the same size — 256 bits, say. (This is the "bit hash" — the fixed length is the output.) The fingerprint tells you nothing about how big the thing it came from was.
  • One-way. This is the important one. You can match a print at a crime scene to a person on file, but you cannot rebuild a person's face — or their DNA — from the print alone. A hash is a one-way street: easy to go from input to fingerprint, effectively impossible to go back.
  • Collision-resistant. No two people should share a fingerprint. Likewise, no two inputs should ever produce the same hash. When a hash function does let two inputs collide, it's broken — hold that thought, because it's exactly what sank MD5.

That's the checklist — one-way, fixed-length, collision-resistant — with determinism sitting underneath all of it. Encryption is about confidentiality (keeping a secret). Hashing is about integrity (proving something hasn't changed, and proving you know it without revealing it).

The part where reality gets stranger than a fingerprint

There's one more property, and it's the one that makes "one-way" truly ironclad — but no everyday analogy prepares you for it, so you should just watch it happen.

In the physical world, small changes make small differences: change one ingredient and the dish tastes slightly off. A hash does the opposite. Change a single character of the input and roughly half of the output flips — unpredictably, with no family resemblance to what came before. password and Password don't produce similar hashes; they produce two completely unrelated ones.

hands-on · Hash Avalanche

Type a word, then change a single letter, and watch the grid: about half of the 256 output bits flip from a one-character nudge. Then hit the identical-inputs preset and watch it fall to zero — same input, same hash, every time.

back to the lesson

What you just saw. Because any change scrambles everything, there is no "getting warmer." An attacker can't compare their guess to the stored hash and see they're close — a near-miss looks exactly as wrong as a wild miss. So they can't sneak up on the answer. They can only guess an input, hash it, and check for an exact match — which is precisely the grind you watched in Part 1. The avalanche property is why cracking is guess-and-check instead of reverse-engineering.

A rule of thumb for telling them apart

When you're not sure whether something is encryption or hashing, there's a clean test: what is the data doing?

  • Data in transit, needing to be read again at the other end → that's encryption (two-way, reversible, confidentiality).
  • Data at rest, that only ever needs to be checked against, never read back → that's hashing (one-way, integrity).

Your password lives in the second category. It sits in storage, and the server only ever needs to check it, never read it back. That's why it's hashed, not encrypted.

Back to the Three Variables — this is where storage meets speed

Here's where Part 1 and Part 2 lock together. When a database is breached, the attacker walks away with hashes, not passwords — and now has to crack each one by guess-and-hash. How fast they can do that (variable 3) depends almost entirely on a choice the company made, not one you made:

  • Stored with a fast hash like MD5, the attacker's rig screams through billions of guesses a second — that's the 47-minute crack from Part 1. Worse, MD5 also fails the collision test, so it's broken twice over.
  • Stored with a deliberately slow, salted hash like bcrypt, the same attacker manages only a few thousand guesses a second. The identical password now holds for centuries. That's variable 3 pushed through the floor on purpose.

And salting is the quiet hero. A salt is a chunk of random data mixed into each password before hashing, so that two people who both picked "Summer2024" end up with two completely different stored hashes. That does two things: the attacker can't crack everyone's password in one bulk pass (each needs its own effort), and they can't use giant precomputed lookup tables. In Three-Variables terms, salt quietly buys back the entropy — variables 1 and 2 — that a predictable password gave away.

The takeaway that surprises most people: how a company stores your password can matter more than how you chose it. A fingerprint machine that's fast and unsalted can undo a genuinely strong password; a slow, salted one can protect a mediocre one. Both are variable-3 moves.

So that's why you hear what you hear

  • "Your password is hashed and salted." Now it means something: stored one-way, with per-user randomness, so a breach yields fingerprints an attacker still has to grind through one at a time.
  • "We've retired MD5 / SHA-1." They're pushing variable 3 down and closing the collision hole.
  • "We were breached, but passwords were salted and hashed with bcrypt." Genuinely reassuring. "Passwords were stored in plaintext" (or fast, unsalted MD5) is a five-alarm fire. You can now tell the difference — and it's the difference between an inconvenience and an emergency.

CRYPTOGRAPHY · PART 3/3

Part 3 — Sharing a Secret in Plain Sight

The other kind of lock

Part 2 was about the one-way street: a hash you can walk down but never back up, perfect for storing something you only ever need to check. But most of what you send all day — a message, a file, a login session — has to be read at the other end. A one-way street is useless for that. You need a door you can lock and unlock.

That's encryption: a transformation you can run forwards and backwards, controlled by a key. There's a lovely, deliberately un-scary way to picture the "series of steps controlled by an input" idea — converting Fahrenheit to Celsius (subtract 32, multiply by 5, divide by 9) is a fixed recipe that turns one number into another, and you can run it in reverse to get back. Encryption is that same idea, except the recipe is scrambled by a key: plaintext plus key goes in, ciphertext comes out; ciphertext plus key goes back, plaintext returns. No key, no reversing.

The entire interesting part of this part is one question: where does the key come from, and how do two people ever come to share one? It turns out there are two answers, and modern security uses both.

Flavor one: one shared key (symmetric)

The simplest version: both people hold a copy of the same key. One key locks, the same key unlocks — like two people sharing a copy of one physical door key. It's fast and it works beautifully... for two people.

The trouble starts when the group grows, and it's worth feeling why before you see it. Picture a party where every pair of guests needs their own private handshake that no one else knows. Two guests: one handshake. Add a third: they need a handshake with each of the first two — two new ones. Add a fourth: three new handshakes. Every newcomer has to shake hands privately with everyone already there. The count doesn't grow — it detonates.

hands-on · Key-Scaling Visualizer

Drag the group size up. Each line is one shared secret. Watch the web thicken from a single strand into an unreadable tangle, and watch the counter climb: a group of 10 needs 45 keys; 100 people need nearly 5,000; 10,000 people need almost 50 million. Then flip the toggle to asymmetric and watch the mesh vanish.

back to the lesson

What you just saw. That contrast is the whole reason the second flavor had to be invented — a shared-secret-per-pair world simply doesn't scale past a handful of people. (And there's a second problem hiding underneath the first: even for just two people, how do you get them a copy of the shared key in the first place, without an eavesdropper grabbing it in transit? Hold that — it's the trick at the end of this part.)

Flavor two: a pair of keys (asymmetric)

Asymmetric cryptography — "public-key" crypto — gives each person two keys that work as a matched pair: a public key they hand out to anyone, and a private key they never share.

The mental model that makes it click: imagine you hand out open padlocks with your name on them, but keep the only key. Anyone can take one of your padlocks, snap it shut around a box, and mail it to you — but only you can open it, because only you have the key. The public key is the open padlock you give away freely; the private key is the one key you guard. Publishing the padlock gives nothing away, because a padlock can only lock.

This is exactly the Bob-and-Alice rule: if Bob wants to send Alice something private, he locks it with Alice's public padlock, and only Alice's private key can open it. (People trip on the direction constantly, so it's worth saying slowly: you encrypt to someone using their public key, not your own.) And because each person only needs their own single pair, the key count grows in a straight line — 10,000 people need 20,000 keys, not 50 million. That's the flat line you saw replace the tangle.

The trick: agreeing on a secret over a tapped wire

Here's the elegant part, and the answer to the question we parked twice. Asymmetric crypto is more secure over open channels, but it's slow — expensive to run for a whole conversation. Symmetric is fast but needs a shared key. So what actually happens is a marriage of the two: use the slow, safe asymmetric method just long enough to agree on a shared symmetric key, then run the real conversation over the fast symmetric channel. When you're done, that channel is thrown away; reconnecting means doing it again.

But that leaves the puzzle from Part 2: how do two strangers agree on a secret key while an eavesdropper records every word they exchange? The color-mixing picture is the clearest explanation there is:

  1. The two of them openly agree on a common color — say, yellow. Anyone listening hears "yellow." Fine.
  2. Each privately picks a secret color and mixes it into the yellow. Alice ends up with orange, Bob with green.
  3. They swap those mixed colors in the open. The eavesdropper now sees yellow, orange, and green.
  4. Each one mixes their own secret color into the color they received. Alice mixes her secret into Bob's green; Bob mixes his secret into Alice's orange. Astonishingly, both arrive at the exact same final color — a shared secret neither ever stated out loud.

The eavesdropper is stuck. They saw yellow, orange, and green go by — but paint only mixes one way. You can't un-mix a color to recover the secret shades that went into it. It's the same one-way property from Part 2, doing defensive work: easy to combine, effectively impossible to reverse. The two parties now share a secret the listener will never derive, and the fast symmetric conversation can begin.

Optional depth (for the genuinely curious). In the real thing (a Diffie-Hellman exchange), "mixing colors" is raising a number to a secret power and taking a remainder — A = gᵃ mod p. Recovering the secret exponent from what's sent is the "un-mixing" step, and with large enough prime numbers it's a problem no computer can solve in any useful timeframe. You never need the math to trust the idea — the paint tells you everything — but it's there if you want it.

The five goals — the lens that ties it all together

Step back, and every piece of this module snaps into a single framework — the five goals of cryptography:

  • Confidentiality — keeping data secret, whether it's at rest, in transit, or in use. This is encryption's job (Part 3).
  • Integrity — proving data hasn't been altered. This is hashing's job (Part 2). Change one bit, the fingerprint changes completely — so any tampering shows.
  • Authentication — proving you are who you claim to be.
  • Obfuscation — hiding the workings of something (like source code) while still letting it run.
  • Non-repudiation — proving who something came from, so they can't deny sending it. We get this from digital signatures, and — this is the key fact — it's achievable only with asymmetric cryptography. A signature is the padlock trick run in reverse: you "sign" with your private key, and anyone can verify it with your public one, proving it could only have come from you.

That last goal is the bridge back to training you've already done. Non-repudiation is the machinery under "verify the sender." When the Phishing & Deepfakes stage told you to confirm an unexpected request came from who it claims — a signed email, a verified domain — this is the math that makes real verification possible, and its absence is the gap every impersonation attack drives through. And confidentiality in transit is precisely what the Security Fundamentals stage was protecting when it warned you about public Wi-Fi: encryption is what stops the person on the same café network from reading your traffic.

Where you already lean on all of this, every day

  • The padlock in your browser bar (HTTPS). Every secure website runs exactly the hybrid dance above — asymmetric to introduce and agree on a key, symmetric to carry the page. The padlock means it happened.
  • Passkeys. They're an asymmetric keypair standing in for a password. The site stores only your public key — and a public key is useless to a thief, because it can only lock, never open. There's no shared secret in storage to steal. (Recall Part 1: passwordless drives the attacker's speed toward zero — this is how.)
  • A VPN on open Wi-Fi. It wraps your traffic in an encrypted tunnel, so the confidentiality you'd otherwise lose on a shared network is restored.

None of these are magic, and none are optional cleverness. They're the five goals, quietly at work.

OSINT · PART 1/3

Part 1 — Open-Source Profiling: The Mosaic

You just spent a whole module on defense. This is the other side of the glass.

Everything up to now taught you why the locks work — why a random password resists a cracker, why a hash can't be reversed, why the padlock in your browser means something. This module flips the camera around to the attacker's chair, and asks a colder question: before they ever send you an email, how do they figure out who you are and what your secrets probably are?

The frame for this is a black-box assessment — the way a sanctioned red-teamer actually starts. They're handed almost nothing: a company name, maybe a domain. No employee list, no org chart, no passwords. Everything else, they have to build from what's already public. So recon isn't a step before the attack — recon is the attack. By the time an email goes out, the hard part is already done.

And here's the connection back to the last module. You learned that a password's strength is a search space — how many guesses an attacker must make. Open-source intelligence is the craft of shrinking that space using facts you gave away for free. Every detail an attacker digs up narrows two things at once: who to target (from a whole company down to one exploitable person) and what their secrets are (from "some password" down to "pet plus birth year"). That's the thread running through this entire module. OSINT is entropy reduction.

The jigsaw

Hold up a single jigsaw piece. A patch of blue. Is it sky? Sea? A shirt? A car? It tells you almost nothing, and you'd feel silly guarding it. That's how each piece of your public footprint feels in isolation: who cares if people know I have a dog?

Now assemble the pieces. Your employer, from LinkedIn. Your dog's name, from an Instagram hashtag. Your hometown, from a Facebook "moving day" post. Your birth year, from a comment on a birthday thread. Your alma mater, from a conference bio. Not one of those is a secret. Not one of those, alone, hurts you. But snap them together and the picture is unmistakable — precise enough to guess your passwords, answer your security questions, and write an email you'll believe came from your boss.

That's the whole idea, and it's the thing most people never see: the danger was never any single post. The danger is the aggregate. Attackers don't need you to leak something sensitive. They just need enough harmless pieces to assemble the sensitive thing themselves.

Three "harmless" facts

Here's the statistic that makes it concrete. A Carnegie Mellon researcher showed that just three data points — ZIP code, full birth date, and gender — uniquely identify about 87% of Americans.

Read that again. Not your name. Not your address. Not anything you'd think twice about sharing. Three details most people consider completely public, combined, single you out of a third of a billion people. That's the mosaic in one sentence: the identifying power isn't in any one fact, it's in the combination — and combinations are exactly what an attacker assembles.

The email in Phishing & Deepfakes didn't come from nowhere

Think back to the phishing stage. The email that almost worked — the urgent one from "your manager," referencing the real project you were stressed about, using the right names and the right tone. You learned to spot it. But you probably assumed the attacker was just clever.

They weren't clever. They were researched. The project name came from a LinkedIn post. The manager's name came from a press release. The tone came from reading your public writing. The timing — Monday, 9 a.m. — came from knowing you'd be clearing a backlog on autopilot. Every ingredient that made that phish believable was assembled, in advance, from the mosaic. This module is the weeks of quiet work that happen before the email you were taught to catch.

So that's why "lock down your privacy" is security advice, not just etiquette

If oversharing felt like a personal-taste thing — some people are private, some aren't — this is where it becomes a security control:

  • Every public detail is a piece someone else can use. You're not deciding whether one fact is sensitive; you're deciding how many pieces of your mosaic are lying face-up.
  • The pieces that hurt most are the ones that feel most harmless — pets, hometowns, birthdays, schools — because those are exactly the ingredients of passwords and security answers, and exactly what people share freely.
  • Aggregation can't be undone one post at a time. Once the picture is assembled, deleting a single piece barely changes it. The defense is upstream: share less, and set your defaults to private.

None of that means going dark. It means understanding that your footprint is a set an attacker reads together — and the smaller and more private that set, the less of a mosaic there is to build.

OSINT · PART 2/3

Part 2 — The Intel Dossier: The Funnel

First problem: a company has hundreds of people. Who gets picked?

Part 1 gave you the principle — scattered public pieces aggregate into a profile. But an attacker staring at a whole organization has a prior question to answer before they profile anyone: which one? They don't build a dossier on all five hundred employees. They build one on the person who will cost them the least effort for the most payoff. Recon starts as a funnel — narrowing a crowd down to a single, well-chosen target.

The predator and the herd

A lion doesn't charge the middle of the herd. It waits, and it watches, and it picks the one animal that's isolated, or limping, or grazing with its head down — the one that costs the least to bring down. It's not cruelty; it's economics. Effort is finite, so you spend it where it goes furthest.

An attacker scans an organization the same way, weighing three things:

  • Access — what can this person reach? Finance, HR, the CEO's calendar, the production systems? More reach makes them worth more.
  • Exposure — how complete is their public mosaic? Someone who posts constantly is a dossier that's already half-built; someone with a locked-down footprint is weeks of work the attacker would rather avoid.
  • Softness — how likely are they to comply without questioning? And here's the one that surprises people: the classic pick isn't the senior executive. It's the new hire. Four months in, they don't yet know what's normal, they're eager to be helpful, they haven't met everyone yet — so a fake "IT onboarding" request or a "welcome from the VP" email lands without friction. New, junior, oversharing, or under visible pressure — that's the animal grazing at the edge of the herd.

You can't change how much access your role has. But exposure and softness are yours to control — and recognizing that you might be the easy target is the first defense.

Now build the file

Once the target is chosen, the attacker assembles the dossier — and this is where you take the chair. You'll start exactly where a black-box operator starts: a name and an employer, and nothing else. Everything else, you'll pull from public sources, one at a time.

hands-on · Investigation Board

A fictional target sits at the center of the board, surrounded by six locked sources — a company page, LinkedIn, Instagram, Facebook, a conference bio, an old breach dump. Pull each one by correctly reading what that source reveals to a profiler. As you do, watch two things: 1. Every fact is stamped with where it leaked — pet: Biscuit → Instagram, birth year: 1991 → Facebook. The board doesn't just tell you facts; it shows you their provenance. 2. The crack-time meter, top of the board, starts at roughly 15,000 years and tumbles with every fact you add.

back to the lesson

What you just saw. Two things worth holding onto. First, nobody handed you that dossier — you built it, piece by piece, and every piece carried a receipt back to a public post the target chose to make. That provenance is the entire defensive lesson: these weren't leaks or hacks, they were shares. Second, the collapsing meter is the entropy-reduction spine from the last module made literal. Fifteen thousand years to instant — not because the password got weaker, but because every fact you gathered erased another slice of the search space an attacker would otherwise have to guess through. The dossier and the crack are the same act, viewed twice.

So that's why your first months are the risky ones

If it ever seemed odd that security training nags hardest at new employees, this is why:

  • The new hire is the softest target, so attackers aim there first. "Verify unusual requests through a second channel" matters most in your first months, precisely when everything feels unfamiliar and you're most inclined to just comply.
  • A complete public footprint marks you as low-effort prey. The more of your mosaic is face-up, the more you look like the isolated animal — and the less the attacker has to work to reach you.
  • The receipts are the point. Every item on that board came from a share, not a breach. Which means the defense isn't technical — it's deciding, post by post, how much of your file you're assembling for them.

Part 3 — The Final Mission: The Crack

The dossier is built. Now it gets spent.

You've profiled the target and watched the search space fall apart. The last step in an attacker's kill chain is weaponization — turning all that recon into the actual break-in. This is the moment the two halves of New Game+ shake hands: the Cryptography module taught that a password's strength is a search space; the OSINT module gathered every fact needed to erase it. Here's where you cash it in.

A wall, or a form

To a stranger with no information, Biscuit#AnnArbor1991 is a wall — twenty characters, mixed case, a symbol, a number. Nothing to grab onto. Brute force has to try everything, and "everything" is centuries of computing. By the numbers, it's a genuinely strong password.

To someone holding the dossier, the exact same string isn't a wall at all — it's a fill-in-the-blank form. The breach dump already handed over the template: [pet] # [place] [year]. And every blank on that form was answered by a public post — pet from Instagram, place from LinkedIn, year from Facebook, even the # from the leaked pattern. The stranger sees an impenetrable wall; the profiler sees a worksheet with the answers written in the margins.

That's the whole finale in one image. Same password. Two completely different objects. The only thing that changed is what the attacker knew — and knowing came entirely from what the target shared.

What you just saw. The password didn't fall because it was weak — by every conventional measure it was strong. It fell because it was profiled. You reconstructed it from pieces the target left lying in the open. That distinction is the entire point of this module — an attacker never needed to know the secret, only to assemble it.

The turn: strength is only the search space you keep hidden

Here's the uncomfortable synthesis of everything in New Game+, and it's worth sitting with.

The Cryptography module taught you that password strength is search space — charset to the power of length. True. But that math only protects you while the search space stays unknown. The moment an attacker learns the structure and the pieces, the exponent collapses, and a "centuries-strong" password becomes a form to fill in. Biscuit#AnnArbor1991 was never weak. It was knowable — and OSINT is the craft of turning knowable into known.

So the defense isn't what people reach for first. It is not "add another word" — Biscuit#AnnArbor1991!Buddy is still a profiled password, still a form, just with one more blank the attacker will fill from your other dog. The real defense is two moves at once, one from each module:

  • Shrink the footprint (this module). Deny the mosaic. The fewer pieces you leave face-up — private defaults, less sharing of pets and places and dates — the less of a form there is to build. You can't profile what isn't posted.
  • Use secrets a profile can't predict (the Cryptography module). A password manager generates qX9#mL2$vK8@wZ4r — a string with no pet, no place, no year, no pattern to reconstruct. There's no form, because there are no blanks that map to your life. Its search space stays unknown no matter how much recon the attacker does.

Do both, and you've closed the loop from both directions: nothing to gather, and nothing to reconstruct even if they gather it. That's why the two modules were always one lesson. Security is about what stays unknown — and your public footprint decides how much of that you give away for free.

New Game+ complete

You started these advanced modules to understand the machinery under the rules. Look at the whole arc now:

  • A password's strength is a search space — the Three Variables.
  • That space is defended in storage by hashing, and in transit by encryption.
  • And it's attacked, from the outside, by profiling — the quiet reconstruction of your secrets from everything you've shared.

Every password rule, every "lock down your privacy," every nag to verify an unusual request is one of those ideas in work clothes. You don't just follow them now — you know why they exist, and why the person who understands the attacker's chair is the hardest target in the room.

That's the mission. Well run.
